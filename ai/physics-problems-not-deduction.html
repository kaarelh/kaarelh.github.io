My claim is that physics problems are not solved using only deductions from statements which are stated in the textbook — in physics (and any other science), our stated theories do not deductively imply answers to the problems humans routinely solve using the theories. A solution to a physics problem doesn’t look like just following valid formal deduction rules starting from propositional forms of some laws of physics. We don’t know how to specify some formal system in which solutions to e.g. problems in basic mechanics can be stated. One central issue is that “common sense” is involved in the solutions, and that isn’t something that the textbook (or any book) specifies propositionally. Common sense is pretty non-propositional — it’s a lot like how we know how to walk but are completely unable to usefully specify in words how one walks. In simple theoretical physics problems, this shows up in particular in solution steps that look like “viewing the situation described in the problem in the correct way”. In some cases, these could be viewed as steps that translate a problem which is stated vaguely (according to the standards of a mathematician) into a problem that is specified precisely. Here’s an example (it’s not cherry-picked — I looked at https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/info/exercises.html and clicked on a random problem):

  • source: https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/info/exercises/bug_on_band.html
  • problem statement: An infinitely stretchable rubber band has one end nailed to a wall, while the other end is pulled away from the wall at the rate of 1 m/s; initially the band is 1 meter long. A bug on the rubber band, initially near the wall end, is crawling toward the other end at the rate of 0.001 cm/s. Will the bug ever reach the other end? If so, when?

It might be interesting to try to solve the problem yourself if you want. I’ll give my solution and talk a bit about it in the next message.

my solution (written like a physicist would) — but feel free to ignore this and think about your own solution instead, if you prefer:

Let’s say that at time t, the bug is at a fraction f(t) along the band. The length of the band is l+tu, where l is the length of the band and u is the speed with which the band is stretched. Letting the bug’s speed be v, we have df/dt = v/(l+tu), so df = v dt/(l+tu). Integrating from 0 to f, we get f = int_0^t v ds/(l+su) = v/u int_{l/u}^{t+l/u} dw/w = v/u log(tu/l+1). The bug reaches the end when 1 = f = v/u log(tu/l+1). Solving for t and plugging in numbers gives t = e^{100000} - 1.

There are weird steps here which are left pretty implicit. Like, imagine what some sort of formal argument checker would think about this argument. How would we try to formalize it so that it would actually be deductively valid? For this problem, we could maybe try to separate a solution into two stages as follows:

stage 1: Setting up a precise mathematical problem (this is pretty much entirely left implicit in the solution above). This might involve saying the bug “is” a point, saying the rubber band is an expanding interval on the real line, saying the band being nailed to a wall means one end of the interval is fixed and the other end being pulled away at 1 m/s means the other end of the interval is moving at 1 m/s, saying precisely what it means for the point-bug to walk at some speed along the band while the band is expanding (precisely what model to pick for this mathematically could require eg imagining a “two-legged bug” standing on a point of the band (which is moving because the band is being stretched) with one leg and taking a little step onto another point with its other leg and realizing that the stretching that happens in the band between those two points during the small step is a higher-order term that can be ignored — we see here that we might require an auxiliary model to set up the main model). This is not intended as an exhaustive list. I think it’s very tricky to imagine a formal system supporting this (often unstated) part of the solution. To the extent that we are using deductions from some “laws”, these are not laws that have been stated in any physics textbook (or any other textbook). One issue one runs into is that there are sometimes assumptions which are fine in one context, but not in another — in one context, we can say the rubber band is an interval, but in another context, it might be curved; in one context, we can think of a point bug, but in another, we might need to think of the bug as being an extended object (indeed, we already did that above when understanding how to formalize the movement of the bug). If we want to make the know-how that goes into this part of the solution propositionally described, we might need some meta-laws about which kinds of statements are fine to introduce in what context, or something? I expect getting something to work here to be very hard; I think creating some formal system which actually works here would plausibly be one of the coolest things of all time in the philosophy of language/science, and it might well just be impossible (because it’s not the sort of thing that can be captured in such a system, maybe).

stage 2: Solving the mathematical problem. This part can indeed be done deductively from the axioms of ZFC or whatever, assuming we were careful on the previous step.

I think one could have the instinct to say that step 1 was only necessary because the problem wasn’t “presented properly”/[stated clearly], but well, problems in science simply aren’t “presented properly” — science is all about solving problems which aren’t presented properly (that’s, like, what makes it not math). Yes, math shows up in science, but most work in science does not look like solving clear problems.

I think solutions only become even less like solutions to mathematical problems as one leaves the realm of crisp quick problems presented in a textbook, moving closer to research. For example, I think the decomposition of a solution into a step of setting up a mathematical framing of the problem and a step of solving the mathematically framed problem becomes less tenable for harder problems, where you instead get into some much weirder dance with many framings/models (we already saw a little of this above), with it being really hard to construct some unified clear picture in which all of them sit. I think it’s likely various other/further weird things will show up as well if one tries to repeat the above exercise with other problems.
